Qatar National Library مكتبة قطر الوطنية

Gov — Culture & Knowledge · Qatar
qnl.qa
Client
Qatar National Library (QNL)
My Role
Senior UI/UX Designer
Via
Mannai Corporation
Year
2024 – Present

The main portal homepage carries the full range of what QNL runs day to day — Events, Featured News, Top Events, Photos & Videos, Top Tasks and Quick Links — on one page, for both public content and the tools staff use every day.

Left — the same homepage template re-purposed as a department landing page, here for Circulation & Borrowing, with its own hero, gallery, shared documents, calendar and quick links. Right — the media gallery viewer behind every “Photos & Videos” module, with a large preview and a scrollable filmstrip of thumbnails.

Left — the full news grid, reached from any “View All” link on the site. Right — a single article opened from that grid, with its own Latest News carousel underneath so a reader always has somewhere to go next.

Left — Shared Documents, grouped by department with a description and a downloadable file for each. Right — the Correspondence Management Module: incoming and outgoing requests filtered by type, entity, channel and status, with SLA due dates and an overdue count surfaced as its own stat card.

A small but deliberate pair: a confirmation dialog in front of the correspondence dashboard before anything is removed, and a plain-language success screen once it is — the same confirm-then-confirm pattern used everywhere a staff action in this system can't be undone.

The Brief

Qatar National Library operates as both a guardian of Qatar and the region's cultural heritage and a modern public library — serving researchers, students, families and people with disabilities, with physical and heritage collections alongside digital resources and community programmes. Behind that public-facing mission sits the library's own operations: departments that need a homepage of their own, staff who track incoming and outgoing correspondence against SLA deadlines, and shared documents that have to reach the right team. The brief covered both sides — a public site people actually browse for news, events and media, and the internal tooling that keeps a large cultural institution's day-to-day paperwork moving.

My Approach

Rather than design a single homepage, I built one reusable template — hero, gallery, shared documents, calendar, quick links, latest news — that every department can stand up as its own landing page, so Circulation & Borrowing gets a homepage that feels custom without being rebuilt from scratch. The Correspondence Management Module reuses the same filter-plus-status-pill pattern as the rest of the system (New, In Progress, Closed, Overdue against SLA) so staff moving between modules never have to relearn how to read a queue, and every destructive action in the system — deleting a request, in particular — goes through the same confirm dialog and plain-language success state, so nothing is removed by accident. Add specifics on your process — the information architecture you defined, stakeholder input, or a flow you redesigned.

Outcome

A single design system carrying both QNL's public presence — news, events, a media gallery, department pages — and the internal correspondence and document-sharing tools its own staff run the library on, for an institution serving as both Qatar's national library and a guardian of the region's cultural heritage. Add a measurable result — stakeholder sign-off, a usability-testing finding you addressed, or client feedback.

The Daman homepage — “Connecting Generations,” with separate Individual and Corporate entry gates, a live News Room, impact numbers (175K pensioners, 108 investments, 122 partner offers) and the GRSIA mobile app, all under the same persistent Sara assistant that follows the visitor through the rest of the platform.

Qatar's pension and social-insurance authority rebranded its public identity as “Daman” (ضمان, “guarantee”) — a persistent virtual assistant, Sara, sits on every page shown here, including this FAQ system, which sorts pension and retirement regulation into nine navigable categories instead of one long list.

The Media Center consolidates five different content types — Multimedia, News, Articles, Publications and Archive — behind one shared template: the same hero, tab switcher, search and filterable grid, so a visitor never has to relearn the pattern moving between them.

The same “grid out, detail in” pattern reused for a news article (left, with a Key Points list and an Article Summary for a dense regulatory topic) and a full magazine edition (right, with its own table of contents and a downloadable PDF).

El Safwa (الصفوة) — Daman's enhanced retiree-benefits programme, offering exclusive partner discounts across categories from cafes to healthcare. It's the most unusual piece of the system: a retail-style marketplace, with filters, sorting and pagination, carried by the same government authority that runs pension calculations two clicks away.

Left — the Archive behind every magazine edition. Right — Contact Us, with four ways in (phone, email, service hours, social) alongside a structured message form and the office location on a map, for the pensioners and staff who still need a human.

The Brief

GRSIA administers pensions and social insurance for Qatar's retirees and workforce, and was rolling out a new public identity, “Daman,” built on four pillars — trust, transparency, care and sustainability — to make a historically bureaucratic authority feel approachable. A large share of its users are older and less digitally native, so self-service content had to be legible without a branch visit or a phone call, across a genuinely wide surface: FAQs spanning nine regulatory categories, a five-format Media Center, a retiree-discounts programme, and every contact channel the authority offers. Add the specific scope you were briefed to own across this system.

My Approach

The real challenge was consistency across very different kinds of content without flattening any of them. For the Media Center, I built one template — hero, tab switcher, search, filterable grid, detail view — and reused it across Multimedia, News, Articles, Publications and Archive, so five separate content types read as one system instead of five disconnected sections. For the FAQ, instead of one long scroll of questions, I organised pension and retirement law into nine audience-shaped categories (Subscribers & Employers, Retirement Rights, Those Who Deserve It, Pensioner's Return to Work, and more) so a visitor narrows to their situation before reading anything. El Safwa was the hardest balance: a discounts marketplace needed the immediacy of a retail experience — category tiles, filters, a “Get Offer” card — while still sitting credibly inside a government authority's visual identity, so I carried Daman's own palette, typography and card language into it rather than borrowing an e-commerce template wholesale. And Sara, the virtual assistant, runs as one persistent presence across every page shown here — FAQs, Media Center, El Safwa, Contact — as a constant fallback for anyone who doesn't want to search or read. Add specifics you can speak to in an interview — your research with retiree users, stakeholder sign-off across departments, or a flow you iterated on.

Outcome

A single, consistent system carrying Daman's rebrand across everything a pensioner, subscriber or employer might come to the site for — regulatory self-service through a categorised FAQ, five content types under one Media Center template, an unusually retail-feeling discounts programme in El Safwa, and a persistent assistant and full contact system for anyone who still needs a person. Helping take a national pension authority from a purely bureaucratic interface to “Daman” is one of the projects I'm proudest of. Add a measurable result — usability-testing findings with retirees, stakeholder sign-off, or adoption after launch.

Saudi Standards, Metrology & Quality Organization الهيئة السعودية للمواصفات والمقاييس والجودة

Gov — Trade, Vehicle Safety & Consumer Protection · Saudi Arabia
saso.gov.sa
Client
SASO
My Role
Senior UI/UX Designer
Engaged via
Saudi Azm
Year
Sep 2024 – Sep 2025

SASO's public service catalogue — one of 20+ licence and permit types the authority issues. This case study zooms into a single card from this grid: “Issue Identity Card for Internal-Combustion Vehicle” (اصدار بطاقة هوية المركبة ذات الاحتراق الداخلي) and its used-trailer counterpart.

Two entry points into the same card type. Left — a locally-manufactured vehicle, looked up by its GSO conformity-certificate number. Right — a used trailer, where the applicant first declares the source (imported / within the Kingdom) and then looks it up by inspection-report number. Both forms lead with one lookup field rather than a blank form.

What that single lookup unlocks: one certificate number resolves into thirteen structured data blocks — certificate, manufacturer, trademark, vehicle model, body, transmission, fuel type, country of origin, country of production, air intake, weights, dimensions, tyres, engine and homologation — plus a 17-character VIN entered as individually segmented boxes, the same way an inspector reads it off a physical plate. None of it is retyped by the applicant.

Left — the trailer's own version of the same record, with trailer-specific fields (axle weight, length, width, height) in place of engine and fuel data. Right — the equipment-registration dashboard an applicant with multiple submissions in flight uses to see everything at a glance, broken out by status.

The full lifecycle an applicant can see without contacting support. Left — a submitted request, tracked through to review. Right — once approved, the card itself appears here with its own number and a live status — Active or Cancelled — so an applicant always knows exactly where a specific vehicle or trailer stands.

The Brief

SASO is Saudi Arabia's national standards, metrology and quality authority — the body that decides whether a product, a piece of equipment, or a vehicle is fit to be sold or operated in the Kingdom, backed by more than 33,000 approved standards and roughly 82,000 certificates issued. Vehicle and trailer identity cards are one narrow but technically dense corner of that mandate: every internal-combustion vehicle and every trailer needs an official identity record before it can be registered, and that record carries dozens of fields spanning manufacturer, trademark, body, transmission, fuel, origin, weights, dimensions, tyres, engine and homologation data. The brief was to design a request flow for issuing that identity card — for both locally-manufactured vehicles and used, potentially imported, trailers — without asking the applicant to manually transcribe a technical record that size. Add the specific problem you were asked to solve.

My Approach

The form doesn't open on a blank page of fields — it opens on one lookup: a GSO conformity-certificate number for a locally-manufactured vehicle, or an inspection-report number for a trailer (after the applicant declares whether it was imported or built within the Kingdom). That single number resolves into every downstream section — thirteen of them for a vehicle — as structured, already-populated data, so the applicant is verifying a record that already exists rather than assembling one from scratch. The one field I did keep as manual entry, the vehicle identification number, is broken into individually segmented character boxes instead of one free-text input, matching how a VIN is actually read character-by-character off a plate and cutting down transcription errors on the number that determines a vehicle's legal identity. Status is carried through consistently after submission too: a request is trackable in the applicant's Requests list, and once approved, the resulting card appears in a separate Cards list with its own number and a live Active / Cancelled status, so nothing about a specific vehicle's standing is ever a phone call away. Add specifics you can speak to in an interview — research method, stakeholder sign-off process, or a particular section of the form you redesigned.

Outcome

A lookup-first request pattern that replaces a manual, multi-section technical form with one certificate or report number, for a national authority whose standards and certificates directly gate what can legally be sold or operated across Saudi Arabia. Being trusted with a piece of that platform, and to work directly on a service inside an institution of SASO's scale, was one of the projects I'm proudest to have been part of. Add a measurable result — request completion rate, support-ticket reduction, or stakeholder feedback.

Jood Eskan — Donation Kiosk

Self-Service & Physical UX · Saudi Arabia
joodeskan.sa
Client
Jood Eskan
My Role
Senior UI/UX Designer
Via
AZM X
Year
Jan 2023–Nov 2024

Left to right — the idle attract loop, one tap to choose an amount, and the contactless payment screen with a live countdown, matched to the physical tap-to-pay reader built into the kiosk below it.

The Brief

Jood Eskan wanted to take fundraising off the screen and into physical, high-footfall spaces — mall entrances, event lobbies, corporate offices — across Saudi Arabia. The constraint was unforgiving: unlike a website, nobody stops to browse a kiosk. A passer-by realistically gives it nine or ten seconds on the way to somewhere else — their car, an escalator, back to their desk — before they either engage or keep walking. The brief was a self-service donation flow a complete stranger could complete, unprompted and with no staff assistance, from a standing start to a confirmed contactless payment, in around fourteen seconds.

My Approach

I treated the interaction as three decisions, not a form. The idle loop plays a short, wordless human story instead of a menu, so the emotional case is already made before anyone touches the glass — the only decision on screen is a single glowing “Donate Now.” Amount selection is five large preset pills (5 / 10 / 20 / 50 SAR, plus a custom amount) instead of a numeric keypad, so choosing an amount is one tap, not typing. The moment an amount is picked, the flow jumps straight to payment — no cart, no review screen, no account — with a visible countdown and a single animated cue pointing at exactly where to tap a card or phone, matched physically to where the NFC reader actually sits on the kiosk housing, so the eye and the hand land in the same place. The screen closes the loop the instant payment clears with a short thank-you moment before looping back into the attract video, so a donor's last impression is impact, not a receipt. Add specifics you can speak to in an interview — how you tested the timing, any iteration on the amount presets, or accessibility considerations for a public kiosk.

Outcome

A donation flow that gets a first-time, unassisted user from a standing start to a confirmed contactless payment in about fourteen seconds and three taps — built to sit inside the same Ramadan campaign that went on to set a Guinness World Record, giving Jood Eskan a physical channel alongside the digital one. Add a measurable result — number of kiosks deployed, total raised through the kiosk channel, or an average completion time you measured on-site.

The homepage — a single search bar over the entire Qur'an, a “Recently read” shortcut back into an in-progress surah, and a Surahs/Juzas toggle over a grid of every chapter, each card set in ornate Arabic calligraphy rather than a plain text label.

The same surah, read three ways from one persistent tab bar: Reading — a traditional two-page mushaf layout with page-turn navigation; Translation — Saheeh International in English, with footnoted clarifications on individual words and phrases; and Tafsir — the Arabic Tafsir al-Mukhtasar (“the Concise”), with the terms it explains highlighted directly in the verse.

Left — the app's core differentiator: tapping any single word of the Qur'an opens a study panel with its own tab bar — Meaning, Tasreef (morphology), Tajweed rules, Qeraat (recitation variants), English meaning, Articulation and Search results — surfacing a full grammatical and linguistic breakdown of one word without leaving the page. Right — the Settings panel, letting a reader personalise the study experience itself: interface language, tajweed colour-coding, one- versus two-page reading mode, arrow-key page navigation and decorative page framing.

The public landing page, surahapp.com — positioning the underlying text itself: a digital edition of the King Fahd Complex for the Printing of the Holy Qur'an (Madinah), Hafs ‘an ‘Asim narration, 1440 AH, shipped across Huawei AppGallery, Google Play, the App Store and Windows Store on top of the web app, with a public API for other developers and a link into the “Surah Encyclopedia” (Surahpedia).

The Brief

Surah (مصحف سورة), built by the Tafsir Center for Quranic Studies (مركز تفسير للدراسات القرآنية), digitises a specific, authoritative printed Qur'an — the King Fahd Complex for the Printing of the Holy Qur'an's Madinah edition, Hafs ‘an ‘Asim narration — around a tagline the product states outright: enjoyment in recitation, and creativity in content at the level of word, verse, page and surah. That's a design brief in itself, since it commits the product to working at four different zoom levels of the same text at once, from a single tapped letter up to a full chapter, without the interface fragmenting into four different apps.

My Approach

I worked on this as part of the in-house UI/UX team through the project's design and handover. The three top-level reading modes — Reading, Translation, Tafsir — share one fixed tab bar and one navigation strip, so switching between an ornate mushaf page, an English translation and a classical Arabic commentary is a single tap, not a different screen with its own layout to relearn. The word-level study panel carries that same logic one level deeper: instead of a single flat definition popup, it's organised as its own set of tabs (Meaning, Tasreef, Tajweed rules, Qeraat, English meaning, Articulation, Search results), so a student with a narrow question — how is this word inflected, how is it recited, what does it mean in English — gets exactly that answer without wading through the others. The Settings panel treats the reading experience itself as configurable rather than fixed: language, tajweed colour-coding, one- or two-page layout and page decoration are all reader-controlled, which matters for a text read the same way by a first-time learner and a lifelong reciter.

Outcome

Designed, built and handed over: a Qur'an study app that holds reading, translation, classical tafsir and a full word-by-word linguistic toolkit (morphology, tajweed, recitation variants, grammar) inside one consistent interface, shipped across the web, Huawei AppGallery, Google Play, the App Store and Windows Store, with a public API for other developers to build on.
